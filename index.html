        How does it work?

        What is this AI?

        It's a convolutional neural network called EfficientNet-Lite0, designed by Google. It has 50 layers and about 4.6 million adjustable numbers (called weights). It was trained on 1.28 million labelled photos to recognise 1,000 kinds of thing, including five breeds of pet cat, eight wild cats, the American alligator and the Nile crocodile.

        Nobody programmed it to look for whiskers or scales. During training it was shown photo after photo and nudged its weights, bit by bit, whenever it got an answer wrong. The detectors you see are what it ended up with.

        What's a detector?

        Each layer contains dozens to hundreds of detectors (also called channels or feature maps). A detector slides a small pattern-matcher over the grid from the layer before and lights up wherever it finds its pattern. Its output is another grid of numbers, shown here as brightness.

        Each layer's grid is half the width of the one before (112 → 56 → 28 → 14 → 7). The grid squares get bigger and the detectors more numerous, so each square "sees" a bigger part of the photo and knows about more complicated things.

        How do we know which way a detector voted?

        We use calculus. For every detector we ask: "If this detector fired a little bit more, would the answer move towards cat or towards alligator?" That rate of change is called a gradient, and it's computed with backpropagation, the same maths used to train the network. Multiplying it by how strongly the detector is firing gives its vote: orange for cat and teal for alligator.

        The coloured "evidence" pictures add up every detector's vote at each grid square. In early layers it's a speckly mix, because edges and textures turn up on both animals. By the last layer it usually lights up clearly over the animal. (The technical names are gradient × activation and Grad-CAM.)

        Is it really seeing "eyes" and "fur"?

        Roughly. Researchers who study these networks have found that early layers respond to edges and colours, middle layers to textures and patterns, and deeper layers to object parts. The labels on each layer are a helpful simplification, though. Real detectors are messier, and many respond to several things at once.

        Try to fool it

        • A cartoon cat, a cat emoji or a toy cat
        • A lion, tiger or lynx (these count as cats here)
        • A lizard, a snake, a dog or a crocodile-skin pattern
        • Cover half the picture, or hold it upside down
        • Hold it far away, or very close

        Worth knowing

        The AI only knows its 1,000 categories, so anything else gets squeezed into the nearest one. It also learns from whatever photos it was trained on, including their quirks and biases. That's why people test AI systems carefully before trusting them.

        Sketch mode

        The photo AI finds pencil drawings hard, because it learned from photos and relies a lot on texture. So sketch mode adds one small extra layer on top of the same network. It was trained on about 3,700 drawings found online: pencil sketches from the ImageNet-Sketch collection, doodles from Google's Quick, Draw! game, and drawings of other things, so it can say "neither".

        Before the AI sees your drawing, it's cleaned up. The background and your hands are removed, shadows are evened out, and it zooms in on the pencil marks. Your score shows how strongly the AI believes it's the animal you said. It's stretched out at the top, so 95 is much harder to get than 85.

        Runs entirely in this web browser using TensorFlow.js, and no images leave this computer. Model: EfficientNet-Lite0 (Apache 2.0, Google).
